Metric depth models must infer the camera from pixels to output metres. We use a digital zoom, a crop followed by a resize, as a free and exact test of whether they do: the focal length is multiplied by the zoom factor s and the depth of every surviving pixel is unchanged. Two exponents summarise a model's response: its depth scales like s^(−α) (ideal α=0) and its predicted focal like s^β (ideal β=1). Neither needs ground truth. Unlike earlier crop-equivariance tests of depth maps, this audit scores the metric scale and the predicted camera against the exact zoomed camera. Across 800 images from KITTI, NYUv2 and DIODE, the widely used Depth Anything V2 has α=0.95, close to reading depth from apparent size alone. Models that predict their own camera close only part of the gap (α≈0.31–0.36) and under-scale the focal (β=0.45–0.65); Depth Pro lies on the line α+β=1 that apparent-size reasoning predicts (median 0.97 per image). Given the true camera, Depth Pro and DA3-Nested become equivariant (α=−0.03, −0.04): their camera-free output follows the zoom, the focal head carries the whole error and places them on that line, yet their depth fits their own focal better than the true one. We explain this failure mode with a zoom–squash ambiguity: a zoomed photo is pixel-for-pixel the photo of the same scene compressed along the viewing axis, which size cues cannot detect. Under it, a regression-trained focal head shrinks towards a prior and β measures the reliability of its camera cue; inverting this shrinkage without labels, training or the zoom factor makes Depth Pro equivariant (α=0.04). Controls, including a real zoom lens, show the effect is not caused by interpolation or detail loss, and a cue intervention shows that for pixels-only models the magnified content, not the lost context, drives it. Finally, a self-consistency fine-tuning on unlabeled images reduces α to 0.05 on KITTI and 0.07 on NYUv2, once a blur-invariance term removes a "blurry means zoomed" shortcut that the plain objective learns; we report its cost on unzoomed and unseen images.
